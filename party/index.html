A letter to Ontario Liberals

We already know what went wrong.

Three times in a row, Ontarians have told us no. In 2018 they put us in the penalty box, and they were right to. In 2022 we were a party trying to be everything to everyone, and we ended up being nothing in particular to most people. In 2025 we ran a better campaign than the one before, raised more money, recovered official party status, and still could not tell a voter in Sudbury or St. Catharines why our leader, and not Doug Ford, could better steer the economy through a trade war.

Read the full letter

After each of those losses, this party did the honest thing. In 2018, we gathered at Provincial Council and put the people who had run the campaign in front of members to answer for it, and after 2022 and 2025 volunteers gave up their evenings for months to interview candidates, survey members and write down what they heard. The three reviews that came out of that work are careful, candid documents, and the people who wrote them deserve our thanks, as does everyone who kept a riding association alive through a decade in opposition with little money, few tools and nowhere near the help they needed.

Read the three reviews side by side, though, and you notice that they are nearly the same document. Early nominations, field organizers, a campaign director in place long before the writ, clean voter data, a plain-language message about the economy, a platform before the advance polls. The 2025 committee recommended, almost line for line, what the 2022 committee had already recommended, and this year the New Leaf Liberals, organizing from outside the leader’s office, reached many of the same conclusions once again.

So our problem was never a shortage of good advice. Our retrospectives are full of it. What they never came with was an execution plan: a timeline, clear responsibility for delivering it, and reporting that members could actually follow. That is not entirely an indictment of the people inside the party, who have had to meet high expectations with fewer resources every cycle, including years without official party status. But it is time to build on the work they have done and actually change how this party operates.

This document is my plan for doing that. It means choosing candidates earlier, developing our platform openly with members and caucus, giving riding associations more resources and a greater role in the party, building our field organization long before the election, fixing the technology our volunteers depend on, raising money consistently, and rebuilding the Ontario Liberal Party as a genuine big tent that can speak to people well beyond those who voted for us last time. The details matter, and the rest of this document lays out how we get there, who is responsible and when it should happen.

Much of what follows is not original, and I do not pretend otherwise. It is what Ontario Liberals have already told ourselves, often repeatedly. The difference is that good intentions and good recommendations have to become obligations, with people responsible for carrying them through and members able to see whether we are actually doing what we said we would do. A party that wants Ontarians to trust it with government should first show that it can govern itself.

There is a part of this that no reform plan can do for us. The leader has a duty to carry themselves, every day, in a way that shows Ontarians we are ready to govern, and members have a duty, once the leadership election is over, to set aside the cynicism and disagreements and work as a team. Power is the force that holds governing parties together. We do not have it, so it takes discipline instead, and the understanding that our fight is not between factions inside this party, or even ultimately with the current government, but for the confidence of a public that wants a happier and more prosperous future for themselves and for each other.

People are exhausted by politics, and for good reason. Our job is not to ask them to care more about politics. It is to give them something worth believing in, and a credible path toward making Ontario better.

None of that replaces a good leader. All of it is what a good leader owes the people who knock the doors. Hold me to it.

— Eric Lombardi

01
Nominating and attracting candidates

A team Ontarians can imagine governing, nominated in every riding by the end of 2027.

Candidates were the strength of both recent campaigns. They were also nominated late, green-lit slowly, and introduced to voters weeks before the vote. The fix is time, clear rules, a deliberate search for talent, and a leader who is transparent rather than playing games.

What the reviews found
What we would do
Prior-cycle candidate files “were not retained on an accessible central database”; the party had no clear picture of who could run where.
’25 debrief
Take a full inventory of every seat and every past candidate, then run a candidate search that builds a team capable of governing.
Candidates were recruited for a “front-runners' campaign” without being told what that meant. The roster was our greatest strength.
’23 review
Attract top talent Ontarians can imagine in a cabinet, from inside the party and beyond it, with PLA leaders brought in early and part of the process.
The green-light process bottlenecked and the Nominations Commissioner “lacked practical authority.” Fees were applied unequally; the tithe remains.
’25 debrief · NL
Give the commissioner real authority, one fee schedule, and a service standard for decisions.
Early nominations are “a critical driver of campaign readiness.” Nominate 12–18 months ahead.
’25 debrief · NL
Nominate half of ridings by summer 2027 and every riding by the end of 2027.

02
How we develop our platform

Built with members, trade-offs made together, published in summer 2027.

Our last two platforms were written late, by few, for Toronto. The next one is developed by members and caucus in two ways, by topic and by region, refined at the May 2027 convention, and published that summer. Then we sell it in every riding until the election.

What the reviews found
What we would do
Candidates “did not know which three-to-five main policy proposals were the most important.” The 2025 platform was not released until after advance polls had closed.
’23 review · ’25 debrief

The platform “completely disregarded non-GTA ridings” and was “overly urban-centric.”
’23 review · ’25 debrief
Develop policy two ways at once: by topic and by region.
Add a policy convention before the narrative is fixed; create a cadence for policy conventions so the platform reflects the membership.
’23 review · NL
Make the May 2027 convention a refinement of work members and caucus have already done, with open debate.
A vast majority of members want to be more engaged between elections; members should be routinely consulted on policy.
’23 review · NL
Give every member a route in: the policy teams, written submissions with an answer, and motions at convention.

03
Rebuilding ridings and engaging members

Members are not a funding mechanism. Ridings are where the party lives.

Many riding associations are dormant, and the ones that work say the same thing: no money, no tools, no say. New members are asked for a donation before anyone asks what they know or what they would like to do. The rebuild starts by putting resources and responsibility in the ridings.

What the reviews found
What we would do
PLAs “felt ignored” and unsupported for two years before the writ; local campaigns were “on their own” and “talked down to.”

“There just were not enough volunteers.” The base is aging; 82% of local executives named volunteer recruitment their biggest challenge.
’23 review · ’25 debrief
Ask new members for their time and ideas first, and give them a visible path from first shift to riding leadership.
Hire field organizers with a mandate to rebuild PLAs. Recommended in 2023, recommended again in 2025, still asked for by New Leaf.
’23 review · ’25 debrief · NL
Put field organizers in every region in the first year.
Every PLA, in strongholds and in ridings the party has never held, should have the tools, central support and “a clear roadmap to victory.”
NL

Members want to be kept informed and consulted; annual meetings should prioritize engagement, with remote participation and lower cost.

04
Technology and operations

People need to see excellence in how we operate before they will trust us to govern.

Before anyone trusts us to run Ontario, they will watch how we run ourselves. Our tools have fallen behind our volunteers, our vendors go unexamined, and too much of how the party works is invisible to its members. Fixing that is not expensive, and it is the most convincing case we can make.

What the reviews found
What we would do
Tools and knowledge are the constant complaint from ridings. Re-evaluate the tech stack and every vendor relationship; the tools have not kept pace with modern organizing.

Lists stale, phone numbers dead, sign-ups “not reliably shared back to ridings,” clicks measured instead of outcomes. “Why didn't we see this coming?”

Hire a campaign director a year out. The 2025 central team was “formalized very close to the election call.” Fill long-vacant roles; publish minutes; define responsibilities.

How we communicate

Lead with ideas. Flood the zone. Make the conversation about us.

Asking people to vote for us to avoid splitting the vote is not a message, and every time we have fallen back on it we have shown a party that does not know where it stands. We should never be in that position again. The way out is a steady stream of ideas, a crisp message at the top and a local one in every riding, a big tent, and MPPs and candidates who are champions of the things that brought them into politics.

06
Fundraising

Money follows credibility. Build the party, then fund it from the many.

Money comes into a party when the public sees it getting ready to govern. People want to be part of a winning team, and organizations start building relationships with the next government. It is not a flattering way to describe politics, but it is true. So the best fundraising plan is a credible party, and the second best is asking less often and for a reason.

What the reviews found
What we would do
Between elections the party “has always defaulted to a ‘starvation posture’.” No permanent professional fundraising core.
’25 debrief
An always-on fundraising plan with a Director of Fundraising and quarterly reporting.
$9.6M spent in 2022 against $13M by the NDP; about $12M in 2025 against $15M by the PCs. A competitive campaign needs about $15M.
’23 review · ’25 debrief
Earn it by building a party people can see getting ready to govern, and report progress against a published target.
Events were priced beyond many supporters; new members are emailed for money and little else.
’25 debrief · NL

Fundraising results and lessons should be routinely shared, with detailed breakdowns of costs and contracts.
